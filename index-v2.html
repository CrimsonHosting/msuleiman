Summer 2026 · Undergraduate research

Getting a robot to stand on three legs

A quadruped standing still balances over the quadrilateral formed by its four feet. Lift one leg and that shape becomes a triangle. If the center of mass is not already inside it, the robot falls.

I wrote the balance controller by hand — no learned policy. It shifts the torso until the projected center of mass sits inside the triangle formed by the three stance feet, then lifts the front-left foot. Jacobian-based inverse kinematics converts foot targets into joint angles, and a safety check between every phase sends the robot to a known stable pose rather than continuing into a state I hadn’t planned for.

It ran in NVIDIA Isaac Sim first, then on the real machine.

  • PlatformBoston Dynamics Spot — our lab unit, Daisy
  • MethodCenter-of-mass shifting, Jacobian IK, staged safety gates
  • ResultHeld the stance on real hardware

Summer 2026 · Undergraduate research

Autonomous flight on a palm-sized drone

The Crazyflie is small enough to test safely indoors and unforgiving enough that weak control logic shows up immediately. Every behavior runs onboard — optical flow, Lighthouse localization, and a multiranger deck, with no laptop in the loop.

The most interesting one is throw-to-hover: you throw it, and mid-air it works out that it has been thrown, spins up, arrests its own tumble, and settles. The hard part was not speed but confidence — a drone that decides it is flying while still in your hand is a hazard, so the free-fall threshold had to be tuned against real throws.

  • BehaviorsThrow-to-hover, obstacle ping-pong, proximity dodge, figure eight, barrel roll
  • SensingOptical flow, Lighthouse localization, multiranger, AI-deck vision
  • AlsoPrinted replacement motor mounts so a downed drone flew again the same day

Isaac Sim on a shared cluster

Isaac Sim expects a workstation with a display. A shared HPC cluster gives you neither, and nobody knew whether Amherst’s FrostByte could run it at all.

With help from IT I containerized the runtime with Enroot, requested GPUs through SLURM, and built a headless rendering path. That let us train on the college’s own A5000 and A100 GPUs instead of a cloud bill we could not cover — and I wrote the setup down so the next student starts from a tested environment.

The reinforcement learning work sat on top of it: 2,400 simulated robots stepping in parallel, converging in about six hours, each with a randomized payload and floor friction so the policy could not memorize one perfectly-known machine.
